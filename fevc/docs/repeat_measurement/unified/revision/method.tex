\section{What repeated wages tell us}\label{sec:setting}

A worker stays at the same firm for three years. In the two-way fixed-effects model, her
worker effect and her employer's firm effect contribute the same amount to each year's
log wage. Once common year effects and other controls are accounted for, differences
among her three wages tell us about her wage variability. They say little about how
variable another worker's wages are, even at the same firm. The repeat-measurement
method in \fevc{} keeps that distinction. It estimates an error variance within each
worker--firm cell that supplies independent repetitions, then fits a model for the
units for which the data provide only one measurement.

This construction follows directly from the leave-out estimator of Kline, Saggio and
S{\o}lvsten (2020, hereafter KSS). For a stayer with $T_g$ years at one firm, write
$y_{gt}$ for log wage and $\bar y_{g,-t}$ for its mean in the other years. First
consider the model without estimated controls. When year $t$ is left out, the
stayer's predicted wage is $\bar y_{g,-t}$. Averaging KSS's leave-out products
within this worker gives exactly the usual sample variance:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q}
 \frac{1}{T_g}\sum_t y_{gt}(y_{gt}-\bar y_{g,-t})
   =\frac{1}{T_g-1}\sum_t(y_{gt}-\bar y_g)^2
   \equiv q_g .
\end{equation}
The equality holds for two years as well as for twenty. To interpret $q_g$ as the
variance of each year's error, the errors must be independent across years with a
common variance within the worker. Their variance can differ freely across workers.

For a mover, the answer depends on what is left out. Under the default, \fevc{}
deletes the entire match with one employer. Several years at that employer give one
deletion unit, whose errors may be correlated within the match. The other employers
offer observations at different expected wages. The program therefore pools
information across movers to estimate the variance of a mover's match. Under
observation deletion, each observation is a separate unit and independence is
assumed at that level. Two years of the same mover at the same employer can then
serve as repeat measurements. It is the combination of the common mean and the
unit of independence that matters.

The distinction matters even in a panel with many stayers. On the six-year Veneto
design, stayers account for 75\% of the rows after matches are formed. Yet movers
account for 97\% of the first term of the estimated sampling variance for worker
variance, and essentially all of that term for firm variance and the covariance.
For the variance of the sum, the movers' share is 77\%
(Table~\ref{tab:real}). The quality of the mover model thus matters for all four
components. How much it matters depends on which workers link firms and how strongly
their wages influence the component of interest.

\section{From wage variability to a standard error}\label{sec:estimator}

\subsection{The sampling variance}
Let $y_i=x_i'\beta+\varepsilon_i$ be the model on deletion units, conditional on
the regressors and the effects. Under match deletion, a mover's row is the mean
log wage of the match multiplied by $\sqrt{F_i}$, where $F_i$ is its number of
person-years. Its fitted mean is $\sqrt{F_i}(\alpha_g+\psi_j)$ for worker $g$ at
firm $j$. A stayer contributes one row per year with $F_i=1$. The errors have
mean zero, are independent across units, and have variances $\sigma_i^2$.
Throughout this derivation the controls and centring constant are known.
Section~\ref{sec:controls} considers their estimation.

The four components are the person-year variance of worker effects, the variance
of firm effects, their covariance, and the variance of their sum. Each is a
quadratic form $\theta=\beta'A\beta$ with known symmetric $A$. Write
$S_{xx}=X'X$ on an identified parameterisation, $P=XS_{xx}^{-1}X'$,
$M=I-P$, and $B=XS_{xx}^{-1}AS_{xx}^{-1}X'$. With leverage $P_{ii}<1$,
the raw leave-out product is $\hat\sigma_i^2=y_i(y_i-x_i'\hat\beta)/(1-P_{ii})$.
KSS subtract $\sum_i B_{ii}\hat\sigma_i^2$ from the plug-in estimate
$\hat\beta'A\hat\beta$. Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ustat}
 \hat\theta=y'Cy,\qquad
 C_{i\ell}=B_{i\ell}-\tfrac12 M_{i\ell}
  \left(\frac{B_{ii}}{M_{ii}}+\frac{B_{\ell\ell}}{M_{\ell\ell}}\right).
\end{equation}
With exact weights $C_{ii}=0$. Every product in the estimate pairs different
units, which is why arbitrary differences in their variances leave the point
estimate unbiased.

Those variances do enter its precision. Write $\mu=X\beta$, $m=C\mu$ and
$\hat m=Cy$. Expanding $y'Cy$ about $\mu$ gives a linear term $2m'\varepsilon$
and a quadratic term $\varepsilon'C\varepsilon$. They are uncorrelated because
the errors are independent and $C$ has zero diagonal. Their variances add:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:V}
 \V[\hat\theta]=4\sum_i m_i^2\sigma_i^2
       +2\sum_i\sum_{\ell\ne i} C_{i\ell}^2\sigma_i^2\sigma_\ell^2 .
\end{equation}
In the first term, $m_i$ measures how much an error in unit $i$ moves the
component to first order. In the second, the weights measure how much errors
in different units matter jointly. The raw leave-out product is unbiased for
each $\sigma_i^2$. Two such products generally use overlapping leave-out fits,
so their product does not in general estimate $\sigma_i^2\sigma_\ell^2$
without bias. Repeat measurements and the model for the remaining units supply
the variance inputs for both terms.

\subsection{The fitted variance outside repeat-measurement sets}\label{sec:transfer}
Under match deletion let $\cG$ denote the eligible stayers and $\cR$ the movers'
matches. Each row of stayer $g$ receives $q_g$. For the remaining rows the model
allows the variance to vary with the worker, the firm and the design. Match
length matters even if yearly variances are constant. If the $F_i$ yearly errors in a match have
common variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, the collapsed row
has variance $\sigma^2[1+(F_i-1)\rho]$. Leverage distinguishes
units on which the regression depends heavily. Finally, a worker's and a firm's
wage levels help predict variability where the worker or firm effect is large
in absolute value and errors can have a large weight in \eqref{eq:V}.

The program fits a scale and then a profile. With $p_i$ and $f_i$ the midranks
of leverage and unit mass, rescaled to $[-1,1]$, the scale regression is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scale}
 z_i=(1,p_i,f_i,p_i^2,f_i^2)',\qquad
 \hat c_z=\arg\min_c\sum_{i\in\cR}(\hat\sigma_i^2-z_i'c)^2 .
\end{equation}
Write $\hat s_i$ for the fitted scale, floored at 5\% of the mean raw
product on the fitting sample. The profile uses the estimated worker and firm
effects, each standardised over person-years and capped at three standard
deviations in either direction. Writing these standardised, capped effects as $\tilde\alpha_i$ and $\tilde\psi_i$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profile}
 b_i=b(\tilde\alpha_i,\tilde\psi_i),\quad
 \hat d=\arg\min_d\sum_{i\in\cR}(\hat\sigma_i^2-\hat s_i b_i'd)^2,\quad
 \st_i^2=\hat s_i b_i'\hat d .
\end{equation}
Here $b(a,h)=(1,a,a^2,h,h^2,ah)'$ and $n_{\cR}$ denotes the number of mover rows.
These equations describe the full basis in a large sample. The package allows at most one coefficient per hundred fitted units
in each step (retaining an intercept in small samples) and drops collinear
terms in a fixed order. Under observation deletion the fit ordinarily uses the units
outside repeated cells. With fewer than 100 such units it uses all mover units.
With $e_i=y_i-x_i'\hat\beta$, the final variance floor is
$10^{-8}\operatorname{median}_i\{e_i^2/(1-P_{ii})\}$.
It also applies to set variances. A nonpositive mean leave-out product or a fit
in which every fitted variance hits the floor prevents inference.

Fitting the profile on estimated effects has a cost. A worker with only a few
years of wages can have an imprecise estimated effect even in a very large panel.
The error in that effect also enters the observations used to fit the profile.
Under Gaussian errors with a common variance the conditional mean of the raw
product given the fitted coefficients is the common variance. With heteroskedastic
errors the distortion can have either sign. The empirical checks in
Section~\ref{sec:evidence} include this dependence.

\subsection{Why the formula subtracts a term}
For a repeat-measurement set $g$ with $T_g$ units let $c_g$ be the weight
$C_{i\ell}$ between two distinct units in the set. The algebra is easiest to
see for stayers under match deletion. For those rows
$c_g=A_{gg}/[T_g(T_g-1)]$, with $A_{gg}$ the worker's diagonal in the target.
For repeated cells under observation deletion the corresponding weight is
$B_{ii}/(1-P_{ii})$. Using the set and fitted variances gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Vhat}
 \widehat{\V}[\hat\theta]
  =4\sum_i\hat m_i^2\st_i^2
  -2\sum_i\sum_{\ell\ne i}C_{i\ell}^2\st_i^2\st_\ell^2
  +4\sum_g c_g^2q_g^2\,\frac{(T_g-2)(T_g-1)}{T_g+1}.
\end{equation}
The subtraction corrects for estimating $m_i$. Even with the true variances in
hand, $\hat m_i^2$ exceeds $m_i^2$ on average by
$\sum_\ell C_{i\ell}^2\sigma_\ell^2$. Summed over observations, the first term
therefore includes the quadratic contribution to \eqref{eq:V} twice. Subtracting
one copy leaves the desired variance.

The last term accounts for using a set's sample variance more than once. The
same wages enter the variance assigned to each row and the influences of the
other rows in the set. Identical regressors give the adjustment its closed
form. For a stayer, $c_g$ vanishes for firm variance and the worker--firm
covariance. The adjustment can matter for worker variance and the variance of
the sum. It is zero for a two-observation set under the stated fourth-moment
condition. A covariance between two components follows by replacing the
squares of their influences and weights with cross-products.

\paragraph{What the result says about misspecification.}
Consider exact weights, a fixed centring constant and no estimated controls.
Suppose stayers' errors have one variance per stayer, zero third moments and
normal fourth moments. Give each mover's row a fixed variance value
$\sigma_i^2+\Delta_i$. Proposition~\ref{prop:mean} shows that the resulting
variance estimate has mean $\V[\hat\theta]+b(\Delta)$, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:b}
 b(\Delta)=4\sum_{i\in\cR}m_i^2\Delta_i
      -2\sum_{i,\ell\in\cR}C_{i\ell}^2\Delta_i\Delta_\ell .
\end{equation}
With the true mover variances the estimate is exactly unbiased. With misspecified
values, a good fit to the average variance can coexist with a poor standard
error if the errors of the model occur on influential rows. Small errors in
the first sum can also leave a discrepancy in the second when model errors
are related across matches of the same worker. The weights change with the
component, so the same variance model can work well for one component and
poorly for another.

For the variance estimate to concentrate around its mean, no one stayer or
quadratic direction can carry a lasting share of sampling variance, and
suitable moments must exist. The fitted-model consistency result also
requires the weighted misspecification in \eqref{eq:b} to be negligible. Its
two-regression application treats the characteristics in the profile as fixed.
The implemented profile uses estimated effects, and some routes use projected
weights. Appendix~\ref{sec:results} gives the exact scope of the results and
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives their proofs.
